If you have ever been to a gym beyond January, you surely have noticed far fewer gym goers in February. Motivation fades fast, not because we lack discipline, but because our brains are biased toward novelty.  The question remains: how can we maintain our new habits beyond the phase of initial excitement?


As we head into a new year, we set resolutions for the year, block time out for our loved ones and our dreams and draw up a beautiful new journal in preparation for dealing with life’s lemons. Today, we will **explore ways to keep up with these goals when the rush of excitement inevitably fades**.


One of the most demotivating and devastating things that could happen when we are on a high of meeting our goals is when we miss our targets for the first time. Guilt rushes in, triggering rumination and internal conflict. Once we consider this a failure, returning to our new habit feels disproportionately harder. Before you know it, you may find yourself wondering what the point is anyway.


Well, the point is that there are probably 300 more days left in the year. So, while missing a few days may feel catastrophic, we have to remind ourselves that a few slips here and there have no real bearing.

---

Most importantly, we are not to blame if we find it challenging in the beginning. Research has shown that **habit automaticity takes time**. Most people require two to five months to reach this point. If you find yourself losing motivation a few days or weeks into your plan, it may be that the goal was overly optimistic to begin with. In fact, most of us tend to be.


Most goals fail not because we are inherently bound to fail, but rather that **we do not account for the trials and tribulations that life brings us**.  As a rule of thumb, accounting for the unexpected means tripling the time you initially allocated. If you find yourself consistently hitting your targets early, you can always revise them later. Life is fluid, plans and circumstances change, so can our goals. 


Another way to stick to your goals is to **set small and specific targets**. Instead of making grand plans, lower the activation energy by choosing something more easily attainable. For example, a small and specific goal would be to read one page a day, while a grander and vaguer goal would be to finish a book every month. This is because frequency and consistency triumph over long, infrequent sessions.

Anchoring new habits to existing ones is another great strategy. Get a bonus if you can tie it to something rewarding. For example, I will read one page of my book while I enjoy my morning coffee.


If you **prepare everything you need the night before**, all that’s left in the moment is to do the task itself, rather than worrying about logistics and decision-making the day of. Returning to the reading example, I place my book on the dining table where I have my morning coffee, so it’s already within reach. For going to the gym, this might mean packing your bag, filling your water bottle, and laying out your clothes the night before, so in the morning, you just have to get dressed, grab your bag and head out.


Finally, it pays to **reflect on the joy and pleasure the habit brings**. When we pair a habit with pleasure, our dopamine pathways activate. This reinforces behaviours that make us feel good, making them easier to return to over time. 

---
 
### Here is how to take action today:
1. Maximise repetition and frequency by setting small and specific goals.
2. Give yourself ample time to complete these goals (x3 of what you think you need).
3. Link the habit to a context or a cue.
4. Do the preparation the night before.
5. Reflect on the positive feelings the habit brings you.
6. Revise your goals at regular intervals throughout the year!
